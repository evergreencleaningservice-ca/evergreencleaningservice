## Match the Inspection to the Agreed Scope

Inspect only the tasks that were due, in areas the crew could access. Bring the current scope, service calendar and any agreed changes. Confirm the previous cleaning window.

For [recurring office cleaning](https://www.evergreencleaningservice.ca/office-cleaning/), separate tasks required every visit from weekly details and periodic work. The [daily, weekly and monthly task checklist](https://www.evergreencleaningservice.ca/office-cleaning-checklist/) can help organize your list, but your agreement determines what was owed on a particular night.

Agree on observable results: no visible food debris on the cleared counter, for example. Check the exclusions too, including dishes, occupied desks, appliance interiors and areas requiring separate access. Keep extra requests separate.

## When to Inspect Shared Areas

Inspect soon after the relevant cleaning visit, preferably before people use the space again. A pre-opening check gives you a clearer view of the previous night's results than an inspection after lunch. Record both the inspection time and any known use since cleaning.

These are **planning examples**, not required inspection intervals:

- After each scheduled visit, a manager might check the kitchen, washrooms and entrance before opening.
- On a selected morning each week, rotate through less busy rooms after their scheduled clean.
- Check periodic work after its actual due date, allowing for the agreed completion window.
- Use a separate daytime check after a busy lunch or event to identify new needs.

For a twice-weekly contract, connect checks to those two visits. The [office cleaning frequency guide](https://www.evergreencleaningservice.ca/how-often-should-an-office-be-cleaned/) helps when occupancy no longer fits the schedule. Check intervening use before reporting an omission.

If a meeting ran late or a room stayed locked, record that uncertainty and ask the account contact what happened. Cleaning quality control depends on understanding access and timing as well as appearance.

### What a visual inspection can establish

A visual inspection establishes the condition you can see at that time: debris, streaks, visible residue, available supplies and apparent damage. It cannot establish that a surface is free of microorganisms or that a disinfectant's required contact time was met. Scent does not prove disinfection.

Public Health Ontario's [2023 visual assessment guidance](https://www.publichealthontario.ca/-/media/Documents/E/2023/ec/environmental-cleaning-implementing-visual-assessment-audits.pdf?sc_lang=en) separates cleanliness from maintenance observations. It was written for health care settings; this office checklist does not adopt its clinical audit standards.

Where disinfection is agreed, review the product and process separately. The Public Health Agency of Canada's [2026 cleaning and disinfecting guidance](https://www.canada.ca/en/public-health/services/diseases/respiratory-infectious-diseases-reduce-spread-personal-protective-measures/cleaning-disinfecting.html) advises cleaning first, using approved disinfectants identified by a DIN and following label directions, including contact time. A next-morning photograph cannot verify those steps.

## Record Concerns and Confirm Corrections

Record the observation first, then agree on its category, owner and next action. These distinctions keep a missed task separate from work that needs different authorization:

- **Missed task:** included, due and accessible; ask the provider to review and confirm a correction.
- **Scheduled later:** not yet due; retain the planned date and inspect after that visit.
- **Restricted access:** locked or occupied area; confirm how access will be restored and when cleaning can happen.
- **Maintenance problem:** leaking tap, broken dispenser or damaged finish; refer it to the building's maintenance contact.
- **New request:** extra work outside the agreement; confirm scope, price and approval before scheduling it.

If timing or access is uncertain, leave the cause unconfirmed until reviewed. Report an immediate hazard through the building's established procedure rather than waiting for the routine inspection review.

Use a shared log with the exact location, task due, observation, inspection time, relevant occupancy and a reference photo where useful. Name the responsible contact and write the agreed correction date and time. After rechecking, record the result, checker and time; keep an unresolved item open.

### Illustrative example: a 6,000 sq ft Toronto office

This example is illustrative, not an Evergreen client record or promised response schedule. The office has two washrooms, one kitchen and cleaning five nights a week, Monday through Friday. Its scope includes wiping cleared kitchen counters every visit and detailing meeting-room skirting on the last Friday of each month.

At 7:30 a.m. Tuesday, before staff arrive, the manager sees dried coffee residue on the cleared counter beside the kitchen sink. Monday's visit ended at 9 p.m.; the manager confirms there was no later kitchen use. The manager sends this record to the account executive:

- **Location and task:** kitchen, counter beside sink; wipe cleared counters, due Monday night.
- **Observation and inspection:** dried coffee residue beside tap; Tuesday, 7:30 a.m.; photo K-01.
- **Responsible contact:** account executive, who confirms with the crew lead that the task was missed.
- **Agreed correction:** crew lead to arrange counter cleaning during Tuesday's visit, completed by 9 p.m.
- **Recheck:** Wednesday, 7:25 a.m.; manager finds no visible residue and closes the item.

If residue remained, the manager would keep the item open. A message saying the work was completed would remain separate from the manager's inspection result.

During Tuesday's check, the manager also sees dust on meeting-room skirting. The monthly detail is due Friday, so it is recorded as scheduled, not missed. The manager checks it Monday before opening and records whether the Friday task was completed.

## Review Recurring Problems With the Account Executive

Review repeated concerns by location, task and timing with the account executive. Compare the original observations, agreed corrections and rechecks. Look for access barriers, unclear responsibilities or increased use before deciding what must change.

Evergreen assigns the same crew and one account executive for inspections, changes and extra work. Our [How We Work process](https://www.evergreencleaningservice.ca/our-process/) explains that contact arrangement. Agree on your building's review schedule and correction arrangements directly; this article does not set a universal recovery deadline.

If a former storage room becomes a meeting room, update its tasks, frequency, access window and start date in writing. Confirm any price change and who approves it. Cleaning quality control should follow the building's current use.

Avoid judging only the easiest rooms, counting every listed task as due nightly or closing concerns without a recheck. Keep factual descriptions instead of labels such as “poor cleaning.” Ask a prospective provider:

- Who inspects, and how are inspections tied to our service calendar?
- Who receives concerns, and how do we agree on correction deadlines?
- How are access problems, maintenance faults and extra work recorded?
- Who confirms the correction and reviews repeated concerns?

## Frequently Asked Questions

<details><summary><h3>How often should a manager inspect office cleaning?</h3></summary>
<p>Tie inspections to the actual service schedule and the tasks due on each visit. A pre-opening check after a scheduled clean and a weekly rotation through quieter rooms are planning examples. Check periodic work after its agreed completion date, and arrange extra checks when repeated concerns need closer follow-up.</p>
</details>

<details><summary><h3>Can I judge last night's cleaning after staff have used the office?</h3></summary>
<p>You can record the current condition, but later use limits what you can attribute to the cleaning visit. Note the inspection time, known room use and any uncertainty. Where practical, recheck after the next scheduled clean before occupancy, then compare that observation with the written task list.</p>
</details>

<details><summary><h3>What should I include when reporting a missed task?</h3></summary>
<p>Include the exact location, task due, service date, observation and inspection time. Add relevant access or occupancy information and a reference photo where useful. Ask the account contact to confirm the responsible person and correction deadline, then record who rechecked the result and whether the item is closed.</p>
</details>

<details><summary><h3>Is dust on a monthly task automatically a cleaning failure?</h3></summary>
<p>Dust on a periodic surface does not automatically mean a task was missed. Check its agreed due date and whether the completion window has passed. If the task is not due yet, record the planned check; if current conditions need earlier attention, discuss an additional visit or revised frequency.</p>
</details>

<details><summary><h3>Does a visibly clean washroom prove it was disinfected?</h3></summary>
<p>No, appearance alone cannot verify the disinfectant, application method or required contact time. A visual check can identify visible soil, residue, waste and supply levels. Where disinfection is part of the agreement, ask about the approved product and label-based procedure separately from the next-morning inspection of visible results.</p>
</details>

<details><summary><h3>When should an inspection lead to a scope change?</h3></summary>
<p>Review the scope when occupancy, hours, room use or responsibilities change, or when repeated concerns show the existing schedule needs adjustment. Confirm the revised tasks, frequency, access arrangements, price implications and start date in writing. Keep corrections owed under the current agreement separate from requests for additional work.</p>
</details>
